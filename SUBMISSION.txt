THE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A ship can turn off its beacon, but it cannot stop leaving a trail.

Since 2022, a "shadow fleet" of ageing tankers has kept sanctioned Russian oil
moving. These ships change names, re-flag to registries that barely exist and
switch their radio identity. Some are now flagged to landlocked countries.

The oil still reaches the market, so oil traders need to know whether that
hidden supply is growing or shrinking. The data to answer that is public, but
it is scattered across sanctions lists and vessel-tracking records that do not
talk to each other.


WHAT GHOST FLEET DOES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
It answers one question for commodity and energy traders:

    Is hidden, sanctions-linked oil supply rising or falling, and where is it
    moving?

- Trend headline: one plain sentence over 12 months of bars.
- Satellite globe: 276 tankers at their latest observed positions, with
  high-risk vessels in magenta and the 24 radar corridors outlined.
- Busiest ports: the export routes appear straight out of the data.
- Vessel dossier: every identity a ship has used, in order, with its recent
  dated activity on the map.
- Evidence matrix: each piece of evidence has its own row with its source, its
  status (Observed, Derived from AIS, Not observed, Unavailable / unknown or
  Model estimate) and the points it adds to the score.
- Radar lens: the Sentinel-1 radar image itself, draped on the satellite map at
  its true position. Slide or blink between optical and radar.
- Radar time-lapse and gallery: 472 radar passes for 133 ships, each one a
  separate satellite overpass you can fly to.
- Historical evidence replay: step through the year's recorded events month by
  month, with no routes drawn and no gaps filled.
- Search by former name: type a name a ship used years ago and find what it is
  called today.
- Models page: what each model was measured against, including the one that
  failed.

HOW IT WORKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Who is in the fleet. The OpenSanctions maritime export has one row per
   source list. Merging by IMO gives 892 shadow-fleet vessels, 772 of them
   formally sanctioned. We rank by how many lists name each ship.

2. What they did. Global Fishing Watch often stores each re-flag as a separate
   record. We collect every identity carrying the IMO, then a year of dated,
   positioned port calls, loitering and AIS gaps across all of them.

3. What it means. An additive score, capacity ranges from gross tonnage and a
   monthly activity series, written to a dated snapshot.

4. What radar shows. We pulled 127,265 Global Fishing Watch radar detections
   for 24 corridors and read Sentinel-1 image chips remotely from Microsoft
   Planetary Computer. We then trained a small CNN detector and a matcher that
   finds each tanker in the image from its AIS position.

5. The dashboard. A static site (plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with MapLibre
   GL) reads the snapshot and is deployed on Vercel. It has no backend and no
   API keys in the browser.

Built with: Python, pandas, PyTorch, scikit-learn, rasterio, MapLibre GL.


THE MODELS, AND WHAT THEY WERE MEASURED AGAINST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CNN vessel detector
  PR-AUC 0.991 and 97% recall at 98% precision on 147 unseen radar scenes
  (classical CFAR baseline: 0.964).
  Measured against Global Fishing Watch's detections: agreement, not ground
  truth.

Same detector, independent test
  PR-AUC 0.974 (CFAR 0.958) on 20 xView3-SAR scenes from 2020 in other seas,
  with 99% precision and 84% recall. It is weak near shore: 71% recall within
  5 km of land, against 94% for CFAR.
  Measured against xView3-SAR labels, independent of Global Fishing Watch.

Tanker-to-radar matcher
  The top target agrees with Global Fishing Watch's own match 87% of the time
  (583 images).
  Measured against Global Fishing Watch's AIS match, which our matcher never
  sees.

Possible side-by-side pairs
  5 confirmed of 90 reviewed candidates. The automatic rule alone is unreliable
  at 10 m pixels.
  Reviewed visually by Claude (an AI model), not an expert analyst.

Loaded or empty from radar
  AUC 0.47 on unseen tankers, a coin toss. It failed the pass mark set in
  advance, so cargo state stays "unknown".
  Measured against voyage-context labels, which are weak.


WHAT WE SHOW, AND WHAT WE DON'T CLAIM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We show which listed ships are active and where. We don't claim any ship broke
the law.

We show identity switches from tracking records. We don't claim to know whether
a tanker is loaded, because free data has no draft readings.

We show value as an upper bound ($64-145 bn a year). We don't claim to measure
barrels actually moved.

We show long idle periods at sea. We don't claim a ship-to-ship transfer
happened.

We show a dated one-year snapshot of 300 of 892 ships. It is not a live feed.

A sanctions listing or a score is not proof of wrongdoing, and nothing here is
trading advice.


WHAT WE LEARNED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The evasion signal is in identity history, not position. Positions tell you
where a ship is. Identity history tells you what it is trying to hide. Radar
adds physical presence, but freely available Sentinel-1 could not tell a loaded
tanker from an empty one, and a test with a pass mark fixed in advance is how we
found that out rather than guessing.


WHAT'S NEXT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Screen all 892 vessels and refresh the snapshot daily.
2. Add draft data from a licensed AIS provider to estimate loaded vs ballast
   state and turn activity into a barrels-based signal.
3. Fix the detector's near-shore gap and extend radar beyond the 24 corridors.
4. Validate the trend against published export estimates with trader users.
